# How much of a holiday destination is Greece for the people who live in it?

*46.6% of residents say they cannot afford one week of holiday a year, against 27.5% in the EU. Eurostat and ELSTAT data show what those who do get away actually do.*

**Konstantinos Kolovos**, Mathematician (NTUA), MSc Business Analytics (AUEB)

*Στα ελληνικά: [arthro-gr.md](arthro-gr.md)*

---

Summer in Greece means full ferries, queues at the tolls and record arrivals. Tourism is the one story the country tells about itself with confidence.

At the same time, one in two people living here reports being unable to afford one week away from home.

The figure is 46.6% for 2025, according to Eurostat. The EU average is 27.5%. Only Romania does worse.

![Inability to afford one week of holiday, Greece and the EU, 2010–2025](../01-adynamia-diakopes.png)

*Inability to afford one week of holiday, Greece and the EU, 2010–2025. Source: Eurostat, `ilc_mdes02`.*

In 2010 Greece stood at 46.3% and the European Union at 38.8%. Fifteen years later, the Union has come down to 27.5%.

We stayed where we were.

## What the indicator actually measures

The question is not whether someone went on holiday. It is whether they **can**.

And the definition is stricter than it looks. The Eurostat manual counts staying in a second home as being able to afford it — and the house of friends or relatives, and subsidised or borrowed accommodation. Time and health do not count as reasons, provided the money is there.

So anyone answering no is saying two things at once. They have neither the money to pay, nor a free roof anywhere.

In a country full of family homes and holiday houses, that changes what the number means. The 46.6% is not an exaggeration. It is the share of people left without either.

## Income explains most of it

Real median income for the typical Greek household is **22.3% below its 2009 level**. That is the worst performance in the European Union. Along with France, Greece is the only country that has not returned to pre-crisis levels.

The six countries that went through a debt crisis started together and ended up somewhere else. Ireland is now 40.3% above 2009, Portugal 25.5%, Cyprus 8.0%. Greece is 22.3% below.

KEFiM, using national accounts data and an entirely different method, arrives at the same ranking.

In other words, poverty is the main explanation and there is no need to look elsewhere for it. The interesting part starts with the next question: **how do the people who do get away take their holidays?**

## Where they sleep

**52.4%** of the nights that residents of Greece spend travelling within the country are spent in owner-occupied accommodation. That is the highest share in the European Union, against an average of 17.5%.

The European comparisons that follow refer to 2024, the most recent year Eurostat has published. The Greek 2025 figures come from ELSTAT.

![Domestic overnight stays by accommodation type, 2024](../eikones/02-katalymata-2024.png)

*Domestic overnight stays by accommodation type, 2024. Source: Eurostat, `tour_dem_tnac`.*

Adding the homes of friends and relatives, unpaid accommodation reaches **76.5%** of domestic overnight stays. In the EU the equivalent figure is 55.3%.

The same pattern appears in trips abroad, where free accommodation again covers 52% of nights. Only whose house it is changes.

The difference shows in frequency too. A resident of Greece makes just **0.58** domestic personal trips with at least one overnight stay per year, against **1.69** for the average European — roughly 66% fewer trips. In nights, however, the gap narrows sharply: 5.8 against 6.8 per resident, only 15% fewer.

Domestic trips by residents of Greece are far fewer, but on average far longer.

And far cheaper. The same person spends **42 euro** a night on a trip inside the country and **106** on a trip abroad, from the same ELSTAT survey for 2025. When the house is there, holidays are long and cheap. When it is not, they pay European prices and stay half as many days.

## Fifteen per cent of trips, half the nights

Trips of two weeks or more are **15.3%** of trips made by residents of Greece. Yet they generate **50.7%** of overnight stays and only **21.2%** of spending, according to the ELSTAT holiday survey for 2025.

A night on those trips costs **21 euro**. On three-night trips it costs **107**.

Twenty-one euro a night does not pay for accommodation in any commercial form. And it is not only accommodation: the figure covers the entire cost of the trip — transport, food, everything.

There is a second way to see the same thing. Not by duration, but by accommodation.

**49.8%** of domestic overnight stays take place in an owner-occupied holiday home, and a night there costs **20 euro**. In a hotel it costs **100**.

Two independent cuts of the same data, both giving roughly half the country's overnight stays, and both landing at around twenty euro.

## What changed in 2025

This year's figures show a shift. Paid overnight stays on the islands rose **1.1%**, while mainland Greece rose **6.0%**.

![Change in nights in short-term rental accommodation by region, 2024 to 2025](../eikones/03-perifereies-2025.png)

*Change in nights in short-term rental accommodation by region, 2024 to 2025. Source: ELSTAT, STO18.*

All thirteen regions gained foreign visitors. Four lost domestic ones: Crete, the Ionian Islands, Attica and the South Aegean. They are the four with the strongest foreign demand.

At the other end, Epirus gained 8.8% more residents of Greece, the North Aegean 8.5% and Eastern Macedonia and Thrace 8.3%.

Whether prices act as the main barrier **cannot be answered directly** from the official data available. No systematic index is published, at the level of Greek regions, of the cost a resident of Greece faces for a domestic trip to each region. INSETE confirms this, noting that "in the absence of data on the regional distribution of total tourism expenditure" it estimates that distribution approximately, on the basis of inbound tourism receipts.

Duration moves in the same direction. From 2024 to 2025, trips of one to three nights went from 23.9% to 26.7% of the total, while those of fifteen nights and over fell from 17.2% to 15.3%. In parallel, trips abroad have grown faster than domestic ones every year since 2021.

All of this concerns paid accommodation. Owner-occupied holiday homes and family houses, which account for the greater part of Greek overnight stays, are not recorded by region anywhere.

## The question that remains

One year is not a trend. The 2025 figures show a direction, not a conclusion.

There is, however, something the data says clearly. The Eurostat indicator measures ability, not behaviour, and nowhere is it published how many residents of Greece ultimately left for a week. We do not know how many they are, and nobody measures it.

What we do know is that those who go away for a long time go away cheaply. And that cheaply presupposes a house somebody in the family bought or built years ago.

**Greek holidays, to a large extent, are not bought. They are inherited.**

Behind the conversation about arrivals, revenue and new records, a different question therefore arises. What will the generation with nothing to inherit do?
